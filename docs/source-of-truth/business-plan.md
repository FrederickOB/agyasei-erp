# 1. Executive Summary
Agyasei Jnr & Company Limited will establish an integrated, one-stop-shop hardware and cement wholesale/retail chain, launching from a flagship store in Techiman, Bono Region, on 1 August 2026. Techiman sits on the primary trade corridor linking southern Ghana to Burkina Faso, Mali and Niger, and is the geographic and logistical centre of the five-region footprint this plan targets: Bono, Northern, Upper East, Upper West and Savannah.
Over ten years, the company will grow from a single flagship store to a 25-outlet network built on a three-tier format — one Flagship, six Regional Distribution Centres and eighteen Community Micro-Outlets — reaching six regions: the original five (Bono, Northern, Upper East, Upper West, Savannah) plus a Kumasi (Ashanti) cluster added to capture the single largest urban market within reach of the Techiman hub. The product range spans everything required for construction: cement, steel and rebar, aggregates and blocks, roofing and finishing materials, hardware and tools, plumbing and sanitary ware, electrical materials, and value-added services such as cut-to-size and just-in-time delivery.
The financial model (companion Excel workbook) projects revenue growing from GH₵ 2,640,000 in FY1 to GH₵ 22,748,000 by FY5 and GH₵ 41,184,000 by FY10. Blended gross margin improves from 19.3% in FY1 to 24.5% in FY10 through an accelerated shift toward higher-margin hardware, tools and paints — funded by a deliberate stock-width investment rather than a gradual drift — combined with procurement and logistics efficiencies. The business turns EBITDA-positive in FY2 and net-profitable in FY3, generating cumulative net profit of GH₵ 10,929,034 over ten years.
The venture is financed with GH₵ 1,772,000 owner equity — GH₵ 500,000 initial capital, GH₵ 72,000 for the contractual 2-year Techiman rent advance, and a GH₵ 1,200,000 drip-feed of GH₵100,000/month across FY1 earmarked specifically for expanding stock level and width into higher-margin, non-cement categories — plus a GH₵ 1,000,000 bank loan at 7% p.a. (5-year equal-principal term). A strict no-idle-cash policy sweeps any cash above a GH₵100,000 operating buffer into additional inventory every year-end rather than letting it accumulate; the loan is kept at its scheduled balance throughout, since surplus cash is put to work in stock rather than early repayment of cheap 7% debt. On these terms, the model returns a 10-year equity IRR of 42.1% and a positive NPV of GH₵ 3,948,423 at a 20% discount rate. The Kumasi expansion is genuinely ROI-accretive — it lifts IRR from 39.0% (20-outlet base) to 42.1% (25 outlets) — but it also deepens the financing challenge: closing cash reaches GH₵(1,501,082) at its lowest point (FY3) before the network's own cash generation takes over from FY6 onward. A bridge facility or supplemental capital of at least GH₵1.5 million, available by Year 2–3, is required to execute the full six-region rollout without a cash shortfall (Section 11.4).
This plan sets out the business model, market context, competitive strategy, phased footprint rollout, operating plan, margin-improvement programme, risk management approach, and full five- and ten-year financial projections. It should be read alongside the companion Excel financial model and investor deck.
# 2. Company Overview & Vision
## 2.1 The Opportunity
Ghana's cement market alone was valued at approximately GHS 731 million in 2025, growing at a five-year CAGR of 8.1%, with the wider construction sector forecast to average 5.2% annual growth from 2026–2029. Cement consumption per capita (~187kg) remains well below the global average (513kg), implying sustained structural upside. The government's "Big Push" infrastructure programme (GH₵13 billion earmarked in the 2025 budget over a 10-year horizon), a 2-million-unit national housing deficit, and continued urbanisation all point to durable, multi-year demand for cement, steel, hardware and finishing materials.
The market is nonetheless fragmented and thinly capitalised at the distribution level: standalone distributors often earn less than GH₵2 profit per bag of cement. Agyasei Jnr's opportunity is to vertically integrate — capturing both distribution and retail margin — and to build a genuine one-stop shop that keeps contractors from ever needing to visit a second supplier.

Vision: To be Ghana's most trusted, most complete construction-materials chain across the northern and middle belt, known for genuine goods, transparent pricing and dependable supply.
Mission: To give every contractor and self-builder in our footprint a single, reliable place to source everything needed for a construction project — at fair prices, with same-day availability, and backed by credit and delivery services that keep sites moving.

# 3. Business Model
## 3.1 The Three-Tier Network Architecture
Agyasei Jnr's business model is built around a deliberately tiered store network rather than a single format repeated everywhere. Each tier is sized to the population and contractor density it serves, which keeps fixed cost proportionate to local demand while preserving the one-stop-shop promise everywhere the brand operates.
| Tier | Role | Typical Size | Product Depth | Count by Year 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 — Flagship | Techiman hub: full range, regional warehouse, HQ functions | 2,000–3,000 sqm | Full range, all categories | 1 |
| Tier 2 — Regional Distribution Centre (RDC) | One per region: bulk storage, feeds Tier 3 outlets, regional delivery hub | 600–1,000 sqm | Full range, high-volume | 5 |
| Tier 3 — Community Micro-Outlet | District-town presence: everyday items, orders for bulk/specialist items | 150–300 sqm | Core fast-moving range | 14 |

This mirrors the growth-share logic of a BCG-style portfolio: the Flagship and RDCs are the "stars" that anchor supply chain economics and brand trust, while Micro-Outlets are low-capex, quick-to-open "question marks" that convert into reliable cash generators once local demand is proven — consistent with the phased rollout in Section 6.
## 3.2 Revenue Model
- Retail sales — walk-in customers and self-builders purchasing at posted prices.
- Wholesale/contractor sales — volume pricing for registered contractors and developers, with negotiated payment terms.
- Contractor credit — short-term (30–60 day) supplier-style credit extended to vetted, repeat contractor accounts, generating a financing-style margin uplift over time (treated conservatively at zero incremental margin in the base-case financial model — see Section 9, Lever 5).
- Value-added services — cut-to-size steel/timber, mixed-load delivery, and (from Year 3+) small-contractor advisory/BOQ support — a low-capex, high-margin revenue stream.
- Government & institutional tenders — supply contracts tied to the national "Big Push" infrastructure programme and local district assembly projects, targeted from Year 3 once track record and balance sheet support tender bonding requirements.

## 3.4 One-Stop-Shop Value Proposition
- Genuine, quality-assured goods — sourced from manufacturers and authorised distributors only, addressing the counterfeit-materials risk that affects informal-sector purchasing.
- Transparent, posted pricing — reducing the negotiation friction and price-gouging risk contractors report with informal suppliers.
- Same-site availability across categories — a contractor can source cement, steel, tools, paint and fittings in a single visit, cutting site downtime.
- Delivery and credit — owned fleet (scaling from 2 trucks at launch) and contractor credit terms that formal-sector distributors rarely extend at this scale.

## 6.2 Revenue Ramp Discipline
Every new outlet is modelled — not assumed — to ramp toward its steady-state revenue rather than launch at full maturity: 40% of steady-state revenue in its first year of operation, 70% in its second, and 100% from its third year onward. This ramp discipline is what keeps the financial model conservative and defensible rather than a straight-line extrapolation of the most optimistic outlets. # 7. Operations Plan
## 7.1 Supply Chain & Procurement
- Multi-source cement and steel from at least two of GHACEM, Dangote and CIMAF to preserve negotiating leverage and supply security (Section 9, Lever 2).
- Central procurement at the Techiman flagship for bulk categories, with each RDC holding regional buffer stock and each Micro-Outlet replenished on a scheduled delivery cycle.
- Supplier vetting programme to guarantee genuine, non-counterfeit goods across all categories, underpinning the brand's core trust proposition.
## 7.2 Logistics & Fleet
The company launches with 2 owned trucks and adds one additional truck with every RDC opened (6 RDCs by Year 7, including Kumasi), reaching a fleet of 8 trucks by Year 10. This scales distribution capacity in step with the network rather than front-loading fleet capex before there is volume to justify it — the reverse of a national 10–15-truck Day-1 fleet, which would be oversized for a 25-outlet, 6-region footprint. Distribution cost as a share of revenue falls from 5.0% in FY1 to 2.5% in FY10 as the owned fleet absorbs volume that would otherwise go to third-party haulage (Section 9, Lever 3).

# 9. Margin-Improvement Strategy: The Five Levers
Distribution-level cement margins in Ghana are notoriously thin — often less than GH₵2 per bag for a standalone distributor. Agyasei Jnr's financial model shows blended gross margin improving from 18.8% in FY1 to 23.6% in FY10 (see the "Margin-Improvement Bridge" tab in the companion Excel model). This is not assumed — it is the deliberate, planned outcome of five levers applied in sequence as the business scales:
### Lever 1 — Product-Mix Shift
Cement's share of revenue falls from ~40% at launch (FY1, used as a footfall/traffic-builder) to ~28% by FY5+, as hardware and tools (30–50% margin) grow from 15% to 22% of the mix and paints/finishes (25–35% margin) grow from 10% to 15%. This single lever accounts for the majority of the margin improvement.
### Lever 2 — Volume Procurement
Direct supply agreements with 2–3 cement and steel manufacturers, negotiated once annual network volumes justify manufacturer-direct terms (targeted from Year 4–5), aiming for a 5–8% reduction in landed cost on cement and steel.
### Lever 3 — Logistics Arbitrage
Owned fleet, scaled in step with RDC openings (Section 7.2), cuts distribution cost from 5.0% of revenue in FY1 to 2.5% in FY10 — capturing the margin that would otherwise be paid to third-party haulage.
### Lever 4 — Brand Premium
The genuine-goods, transparent-pricing positioning (Section 8.1) supports a 3–5% price premium over unbranded/roadside sellers on hardware, tools and finishes once brand trust is established locally (typically from each outlet's Year 3 of operation).
### Lever 5 — Financial Engineering
Contractor credit financed against contractor deposits, plus a 2–3%/month credit facility for approved accounts, creates a financing-style margin on top of product margin — modelled conservatively at zero incremental fee income, so it represents unmodelled upside on the revenue side. Its modelled effect is instead on working capital efficiency: extending credit is a demand-pull strategy that moves stock faster, so the financial model reduces Inventory Days from 45 in FY1 to 28 by FY10 as the credit facility and the network's ERP/POS visibility mature — inventory turns roughly 38% faster by FY10 than under a static assumption, freeing up cash that would otherwise sit in stock.
## 9.1 Break-Even Discipline
The Techiman flagship alone requires GH₵ 2,880,000 in annual revenue to cover its fixed staff and rent costs at FY1 contribution margin — against a modelled FY1 revenue of GH₵ 2,400,000 (the store is still on its 40% revenue ramp in Year 1, per Section 6.2). Network-wide, the model shows EBITDA turning positive in FY2 and net profit turning positive in FY4, once enough outlets have moved past their first-year ramp and the margin-mix shift has taken hold. Full break-even mechanics for every fiscal year are in the "Returns & Break-Even" tab of the companion Excel model.

## 11.4 Cash Position — The Bridge-Financing Requirement
The GH₵1,772,000 Year-1 package, combined with the no-idle-cash sweep, covers FY1 (closing cash of GH₵ 100,000, the operating buffer). But the six-region rollout — now including the Kumasi cluster — draws the balance down sharply from FY2. Closing cash reaches its lowest point in FY3 at GH₵ -1,393,958, before recovering through FY6 and holding at the GH₵100,000 buffer from FY6 onward as the sweep policy redirects all further surplus into inventory rather than a growing cash balance. The faster inventory turnover from the contractor-credit strategy (Section 9, Lever 5) eases this trough by roughly GH₵107,000 versus a static working-capital assumption, but does not eliminate it.

This is the single most important funding milestone in the plan: a bridge facility or supplemental capital injection of at least GH₵ 1,400,000 — sized to the FY3 trough — is required, available by Year 2, for the business to execute the full 25-outlet, six-region rollout without a cash shortfall. This is larger and lands earlier than under the original 20-outlet plan, because the Kumasi cluster adds capex in the same window the network is already stretched — though improved inventory turnover (Lever 5) has since narrowed the gap slightly. It is not currently included in the GH₵1,772,000 Year-1 package modelled above, and — under the strict no-idle-cash policy — cannot be self-funded by simply contributing more FY1 equity beyond what the stock-width programme calls for, since any surplus above the GH₵100,000 buffer is swept into stock immediately rather than held as a cash cushion (see the companion Excel model's Sensitivity tab). Because the underlying business is profitable and cash-generative from FY6, this is a timing and bridge-financing question rather than a viability question — but it must be planned for, not discovered.
